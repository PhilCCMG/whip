# 10: Voice and copy

WHIP is **satire**. Its tone is dry and knowing, with an affectionate contempt for the system and for the
people trying to climb it. The touchstones are *The Thick of It*, *Yes Minister*, *Private Eye*,
*The Day Today*, and the British local paper. The game is funny because it takes politics seriously
enough to notice how absurd it is.

## Rules

1. **Punch at institutions, archetypes and the player's own ambition.** Do not punch at real people,
   real parties, or real groups of people. Everyone named is fictional, as the legal notice says.
   Never name or obviously caricature a real politician, journalist or public figure.
2. **Deadpan beats wacky.** The joke is the gap between the official register and the reality:
   "The Chief Whip has offered his full support, which colleagues described as 'ominous'."
   Avoid exclamation marks, "lol", emoji and meme formats.
3. **British register.** Use British spelling and idiom: "colleagues", "the tea room",
   "a source close to", "not ruling anything out", "a spokesperson declined to comment", "council
   tax", "bin collections", "a parish council".
4. **Satirise power, process and spin.** Good targets: euphemism, U-turns dressed as listening,
   leaks, anonymous briefings, focus groups, reshuffles, "lessons will be learned", the bill nobody
   read, hustings in a wet village hall, the constituency surgery, fundraising dinners.
5. **Off-limits.** Real tragedies, disasters or crimes; jokes whose target is a protected
   characteristic; sexual content; graphic violence; mockery of real religions or ethnic groups; and
   anything that reads as partisan propaganda for a real-world party or position. Keep every
   fictional party fair game.
6. **Mechanics must stay legible.** Flavour wraps information but never hides it. A turn report can
   say "Kemp 'deeply relaxed' about rival's promotion", but the numbers and outcomes beside it must
   be clear. Action labels stay functional, such as "Meet politician"; jokes go in descriptions,
   subtitles and confirmations.
7. **Be brief.** Headlines run to 12 words or fewer. Standfirsts are one sentence. UI microcopy is
   one line.
8. **Players will read it a thousand times.** Vary templates, avoid catchphrases that wear thin, and
   draw from pools that rotate deterministically, using the seeded RNG in the simulation and a stable
   hash in the UI.

## The newspaper ("The Week in Westminster" turn report)

- Feel like a real UK paper: a masthead with a fictional title, a price, an edition and date line,
  section labels, bylines ("By our Political Editor"), standfirsts, a lead story, a "Politics" column,
  and a back section of **non-political filler**: "And Finally", "Around the Constituencies",
  "Letters", "Weather", "Sport", "Classifieds", "Corrections".
- **Most of the paper is about this week's game politics**: divisions, rebellions, appointments,
  scandals and the player's own moves, written with spin. Filler is **around a quarter** of the
  stories.
- Filler is funny, harmless, very British local news: a marrow that wins an award, a parish council
  feud, an escaped llama, a pothole gaining "local landmark" status, a lost cat that comes home
  in a cabinet minister's ministerial car. Where possible, use the world's own fictional constituency
  names and politicians ("Ashby & Wold councillor denies being 'the marrow'").
- Fictional titles have different leanings and voices. Examples: *The Morning Whip* (broadsheet),
  *The Daily Clarion* (red-top), *The Westminster Gazette* (paper of record). Don't parody real
  mastheads.
- The paper may comment on the player, by name, according to their reputation with the media.

## Microcopy examples

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| "No notifications" | "Your inbox is empty. Enjoy it while it lasts." |
